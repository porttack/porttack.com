The parts of a seminar that stay the same from topic to topic: the norms, the fishbowl, the boundaries, and a one-sheet handout. Each seminar lesson plan has its own reading and questions, and links here for the rest.


## The run of show

Scaled to whatever time you have. Rough minutes:

1. **Norms, out loud (5 min).** Post them, read them, hold to them.
2. **Opening temperature check (3 min).** Students vote with their feet, going to one side of the room or the other, each side with a position. Note where the room sits, then they return to their seats.
3. **Opening round (4 min).** One question, and every student answers briefly from their entry ticket. No one has to be brave to go first, because everyone goes.
4. **The seminar (20 min).** Fishbowl with the hot seat. I stay mostly silent. Stretch and swap the center group once if the energy earns it.
5. **Closing temperature check (4 min).** Students pick a side again. Who shifted, and what moved them?
6. **Reveal and exit ticket (10 min).** I share my take as one opinion among many, and they write.

A seminar with a second reading adds a round between steps 4 and 5. Its lesson plan says where.

**Why a temperature check.** It is borrowed from Oxford-style debate, where the room is polled before and after and the winner is whoever changed the most minds. The point is not to win. It is to be moved.

## Five norms to post

1. One voice at a time.
2. Build on what came before ("Adding to what ___ said...").
3. Cite the text or a real example when you make a claim.
4. Disagree with the idea, not the person.
5. Invite quiet voices in. Silence is thinking time, not dead air.

The last one is aimed at me. The instinct is to fill every pause, and filling pauses is what kills student talk.

## The fishbowl, with guardrails

A small group of students in the center talk. I use four to six, but use whatever fits your room. One extra chair at the table stays empty: the hot seat. It lets an observer from the outer circle join the conversation for a moment, sit down, make a comment, then get up and leave. Everyone else is on the outside as an observer, with the observer organizer. The outer circle is not the bench. Listening well is the second skill of the day, and the organizer is what makes it visible and gradable.

Three guardrails:

- **Control the chair the first time.** An open invitation becomes a stampede. The first time, an observer raises a hand, I wave them into the hot seat, they make their comment, and they get up and go back to the outer circle. Loosen it once they can handle it.
- **Stack the first circle.** For the debut, hand-pick students who will set the bar rather than clown. Say out loud that the circle rotates all year, so it does not read as favoritism.
- **Keep the observer job simple.** The first time, the task is concrete and non-social. Save partner critique for later.

My job in the center is small: protect the silence, and reach for a back-pocket question only when the room stalls.

## Boundaries, said once

I will have a student or two who would rather be the show than join it. Feeding that with a back-and-forth in front of an audience is the worst move, because the audience is the reward. So I set the boundary once, out loud, at the start, and then enforce it without a scene.

The fishbowl gives a calm ladder. A seat in the circle is a privilege, not a right. Break a norm to grab attention and the first consequence is quiet and automatic: you move out to the observer seats and pick up the organizer. If it continues, you step outside and rejoin when you are ready to work. Because I said all of it up front, enforcing it is not a punishment invented on the spot. It is just what I told them would happen, which drains the drama and denies the attention that was the point.

The goal is not to be harsh. It is to protect the students who came to think from the one who came to perform.

## Playing the other side

Three moves that make students argue the strongest version of a side they do not hold. Use one, two, or all three.

**1. The best argument for the other side (every seminar).** The entry ticket's fourth question asks for it: "What is the best argument for the side you did not pick? Say it so well that someone who holds that view would agree you got it right." Everyone prepares the other side before they speak, so no one has to be assigned a side, and it works when the room is lopsided. In the fishbowl, ask: "Who heard a strong argument for the side you didn't pick?"

**2. Grade it.** This is the line on the handout worth grading closely: how well thought out it is, not whether the student agrees with it. A simple rubric:

| | Strong | Developing | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fair** | Stated the way someone who holds the view would state it | Mostly fair, but a little weaker than the real argument | A caricature, or the student's own side restated |
| **Specific** | Cites a paragraph, a quote, or a detail from the reading | General, with little support from the reading | No support |
| **Reasoned** | Explains why the argument matters, not only what it is | Gives a reason but does not connect it | A bare claim |

Adjust it to your gradebook. The rest of the handout is still graded for effort and honesty.

**3. Assigned defenders (optional, when the room is lopsided).** Choose one or two students to argue the side most of the room does not hold, in the fishbowl. The temperature check already ranks the room: the split is the data. Ask for volunteers first. If nobody volunteers, ask students from the larger side, who are then arguing against their own pick. Do not pick the most outspoken. Use it in a later round, such as the swap in Round 2, and not the first, so the first discussion stays authentic. Give each defender a role card with a few points from the reading ([print blank role cards](/socratic-seminar-cards/); a lesson plan links them already filled in). Say out loud who is playing a role. Afterward, ask what it was like to argue the side they did not hold. The optional second exit-ticket line asks it.

## The handout

One sheet, two sides, with name and period at the top: <a id="handout-link" href="/socratic-seminar-handout/"><strong>Print the handout</strong></a>. Print double-sided and flip on the long edge.

- **Front: the entry ticket.** Filled out after the reading, before the seminar starts. Four prompts: two questions the reading raises for you, ones you could actually argue about; a starting side, and why ("Changing your mind during the seminar is not losing. It is the point."); two pieces of evidence from the reading, by paragraph number; and the best argument for the side you did not pick (see Playing the other side).
- **Back, top: the observer organizer.** The outer circle fills it in while watching; it is their ticket into the hot seat. Four things to catch: an idea they agreed with, an idea they would push back on, the strongest point made, and a question nobody has asked yet. Under it, a tally of each time someone builds on another person's idea instead of starting over. That tally is the difference between a discussion and a set of speeches.
- **Back, bottom: the exit ticket.** "Did your thinking move today? Three sentences: what you believed walking in, what you believe now, and what said or asked in the room moved you (or held you in place)."

A seminar can add one question to the entry ticket (printed as question 5) and up to two to the exit ticket, to check a specific goal. Each lesson plan links to the handout with its own questions filled in. If you reached this page from a lesson plan, the link above already carries that seminar's topic and questions. You can also type your own on the handout page before printing.

I grade effort and honesty here, not the position landed on. "I changed my mind because of what someone said" is the best possible answer. The one line worth grading closely is the best-argument question; the rubric is under Playing the other side.

## Evidence the format produces

Every seminar collects the same artifacts, and none of them needs a separate quiz:

- **Entry ticket:** a starting position, evidence cited by paragraph number, and the best argument for the other side.
- **Observer organizer:** what the outer circle caught, and the tally of times a student built on someone else's idea.
- **Temperature checks:** a count of who moved, and why, from the before-and-after (or three-point) checks.
- **Exit ticket:** what they believed, what they believe now, and what moved them.

Each lesson plan says which of these shows which of its learning goals.

## Common Core and California mandates

Common Core ELA (California adoption). Paraphrased; confirm exact wording against the official documents before citing formally.

- **SL.6.1 / SL.9-10.1:** engage in, and at 9th drive, collaborative discussions, building on others and justifying one's own views. The spine of the activity.
- **SL.6.3 / SL.9-10.3:** delineate and evaluate a speaker's argument, reasoning, and evidence. The observer's job.
- **RI.6.1 / RI.9-10.1:** cite textual evidence to support analysis. The entry ticket and the cite-the-text norm.
- **RI.6.8 / RI.9-10.8:** trace and evaluate an argument and its specific claims. The seminar in motion.
- **W.6.1 / W.9-10.1:** write to support a claim with reasons and evidence. Entry and exit tickets.

**Media literacy (AB 873).** AB 873 (Berman), Chapter 815, Statutes of 2023, adds Education Code Section 33548 and took effect January 1, 2024. It directs the Instructional Quality Commission to weave media literacy and digital citizenship into the ELA, math, science, and history-social science frameworks as they are revised. A seminar on a current technology story is a concrete piece of that intent: students evaluating the claims made about a technology and weighing what it gains or costs them. It is a framework directive rather than a per-lesson standard, so it belongs here as alignment.
