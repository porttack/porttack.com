Every year I start with a digital citizenship unit, and every year the AI conversation goes the same way. I say something about academic honesty. Students nod. Nothing happens.

This year I have a better artifact.

In July, ninety-eight high school students from all fifty states met in the replica Senate chamber at the Edward M. Kennedy Institute in Boston and wrote a national AI policy for K-12 schools. Forty-nine provisions, five sections, passed by a lopsided vote, now on its way to about ten thousand superintendents. It is a real primary source, written by people the same age as the students in the room, and it is short.

That changes the conversation completely. It is no longer a teacher telling students what the rules should be. It is students reading what other students decided, and being asked whether they agree.

Here is the seminar I am running. Take it, change it, tell me what broke.

**Format:** one class period, 50 minutes. Same structure I used for [the Anthropic seminar]({{ site.baseurl }}/teaching) earlier this year. No sides, no debate, no winner. Questions only, and students build on each other.

## The reading packet

Do not hand out the whole bill. Nine pages of legislative prose will lose half the room in four minutes. Print these seven provisions, verbatim, on one page. It is about four hundred words and it contains the entire argument.

**From Section 1, General Provisions**

> 2. Educators should always prioritize educator-student relationships by omitting AI usage in situations where human connection is irreplaceable in the learning environment.

**From Section 2, Student Provisions**

> 1. Students should have the right to appeal any convictions of wrongful AI use. They may be subject to technologies such as AI detectors. Students should prepare their documents to defend against potential false positives, such as procuring the document history of major assignments.

> 4. Students should never use AI for the act of writing, though editing, brainstorming, and studying with AI is permitted after eighth grade. How to edit written assignments (without AI assistance) should be taught and assessed thoroughly prior to eighth grade before students use AI for editing.

> 9. AI use is strictly prohibited on all graded assessments. If a teacher catches a student using it for that purpose, the grade on that assignment must result in a zero unless the teacher specifies otherwise.

> 12. Verification of Mastery: Students must be prepared to demonstrate independent mastery of learning objectives through in-person discussions, handwritten assessments, or oral defenses if AI was utilized during the drafting phase.

**From Section 4, Teachers**

> 2. Teachers must use one standardized AI detector in conjunction with their school district for written graded assessments and use Lockdown browsers for graded online assessments.

**From Section 5, Administrators**

> 8. Repeat Violations. If a student is found to repeatedly misuse AI tools or intentionally break established rules after an initial warning, they shall be subject to progressive discipline, which may include: A. Mandatory parent-teacher-administrator conferences. B. Requirement to redo the assignment in a strictly monitored, non-digital environment. C. Loss of credit for the assignment or course. D. Academic probation or other disciplinary actions as defined by the school's code of conduct.

## The question sequence

Five stages, roughly ten minutes each. Ask the question, then stop talking. The silence is the lesson.

**Opening. What do you notice?**

These rules were written by students, in one weekend, from every state. Nobody made them write any of this. Read the packet. What surprised you?

*Let this run long. It is the only question with no agenda.*

**Core. Go deeper.**

Provision 4 says never use AI for the act of writing, but editing and brainstorming are fine after eighth grade. And it says editing has to be taught and assessed first. Why eighth grade? What has to happen before the tool is safe?

*This is the provision I most want them to sit with. Push on "what has to happen first."*

**Core. Challenge assumptions.**

Provision 2 in Section 4 requires every teacher to use one standardized AI detector. Provision 1 in Section 2 tells you to save your document history so you can defend yourself against false accusations by that detector. What does the second rule tell you about the first? Why would someone write both?

*If nobody gets there, ask: who is protected by rule one, and who is protected by rule two?*

**Synthesis. Count.**

Go through the packet and sort each provision into one of three piles. Does it catch someone, does it protect someone from being caught, or does it make someone able to do something new? What is the ratio? What does that ratio tell you about what school is for?

*This is where the interactive below is useful. Project it.*

**Apply. Now you try.**

You get one weekend and a blank page. Write three rules for AI in this school. Not about catching anyone. About what you should be able to do by the time you graduate.

*Collect these. They are the artifact. Mine went on the wall.*

I built this to project during the Synthesis question. It has all forty-nine provisions of the bill. Students sort them, the tally updates live, and my classification is hidden until they commit to their own.

The point is not to agree with me. Several provisions are genuinely arguable, and the arguing is the good part.

## Facilitation notes

**On the detector question.** Somebody will ask whether AI detectors actually work. The honest answer is that they do not work well enough to accuse a specific student, and the reason is arithmetic rather than software quality. If you want to run that math with the class, I built a calculator for it in [the companion piece]({% post_url 2026-08-07-the-kids-wrote-a-discipline-policy %}). In a CS class it doubles as a genuinely good conditional probability lesson.

**On the temptation to editorialize.** I have strong opinions about this bill and I wrote them up separately, on purpose. In the seminar I keep them out of the room. If students ask me directly, I say I have written about it and they can read it after.

**On the kid who says the rules are too harsh.** Follow up with: who wrote them? Let them sit with the fact that it was students.

**On timing.** The Apply question needs its full ten minutes and it is the one that always gets squeezed. If you are running behind, cut the Opening short, not the close.

**On the eighth-grade provision, if you teach middle school.** Provision 4 says editing has to be taught and assessed before eighth grade. Ask your middle schoolers whether that has happened to them. The answer in my room was instructive.
