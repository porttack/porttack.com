Ninety-eight high school students wrote a national AI policy for schools this summer. Before I tell my students what I think of it, they read some of it and argue about it.

The format is in the [Socratic Seminar Kit](/socratic-seminar-kit/?topic=The%20Rules%20Students%20Wrote&entry=Which%20rule%20would%20you%20cut%2C%20and%20what%20would%20you%20put%20in%20its%20place%3F&exit=Write%20one%20rule%20you%20would%20cut%2C%20change%2C%20or%20add%2C%20and%20say%20why.): the norms, the fishbowl, and the three tickets. This page has the reading and questions. My own take on the bill is in [The Kids Wrote a Discipline Policy]({% post_url 2026-08-07-the-kids-wrote-a-discipline-policy %}). I keep it out of the room until the end.

This reading works as a re-introduction because the text is short and every student already has an opinion about rules written about them.

Slides: **[The Rules Students Wrote](/students-first-slides/)**

## Learning goals

By the end of the seminar, students can:

1. Weigh the beneficial and harmful effects of AI tools in school, and of the rules written to control them, and defend a position with evidence from the bill. AP CSP: [IOC-1.A](https://learn.porttack.com/standards/apcsp-standards-reference.html#IOC-1.A), [IOC-1.B](https://learn.porttack.com/standards/apcsp-standards-reference.html#IOC-1.B).
2. Describe the legal and ethical questions a rule like "any unapproved AI use is plagiarism" raises, and who should decide. AP CSP: [IOC-1.F](https://learn.porttack.com/standards/apcsp-standards-reference.html#IOC-1.F).
3. Propose a rule, or a revision, for responsible use of AI tools, and justify it. AP CSP practice: [P6 Responsible Computing](https://learn.porttack.com/standards/apcsp-standards-reference.html#P6).
4. Discuss as a group: cite the text, build on what others said, and change a position when the evidence changes.

## Setup for this seminar

- **Temperature check.** One wall: "Rules like these protect students." Other wall: "Rules like these mostly punish students." Same line, same before-and-after movement.
- **Entry ticket.** The handout prints this extra question: "Which rule would you cut, and what would you put in its place?"
- **Outer circle.** One observer per round listens for anyone who points to a specific rule letter (A to F) instead of "the rules" in general.

## Facilitator questions

Back-pocket only. Open with one.

**Opening (pick one):**

- Rule B says a student can be suspended or expelled if a *parent* uses AI on their homework. Fair, or backwards?
- These are the rules students wrote about themselves. Are they tougher or softer than you expected?

**Core:**

- Rule A treats any AI use that is not explicitly allowed as plagiarism. Who is responsible for saying what is allowed?
- Rule E tells students to save their document history to prove they did not cheat. What does that say about who is trusted?
- Rule C draws an age line: no AI for editing before eighth grade, and never for "the act of writing." Is eighth grade the right line? Is writing code "the act of writing"?
- Rule D puts a detector on every written assessment and a lockdown browser on every online one. Who pays the cost when a detector is wrong?
- Rule F says every student gets a school AI that is "filtered and monitored." Does monitoring make it safer, or make you use it less?
- Of the six, which one would you want to live under? Which one would you fight?

**Deeper:**

- The students wrote forty-nine rules. Most are about catching people. What would a rule look like that helped someone learn instead?
- What rule is missing?

**Closing (optional):**

- Section 5, provision 10 of the same bill says: "Administrators must consult with students, staff, and families before drafting AI rules." Has anyone asked you? What would you tell them?

**After the reveal:**

- Where am I wrong?

## Evidence of learning

All of this is collected during the seminar. Nothing needs a separate quiz.

| Goal | Evidence | What meeting it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Effects | Entry ticket: a starting side and two pieces of evidence by paragraph number. | Names at least one benefit and one harm of a specific rule (A to F) and cites the text. |
| 2. Legal and ethical questions | Fishbowl talk on Rule A and Rule B, tracked on the observer organizer. | Raises a question of fairness or responsibility (who decides what is "permitted"? who is punished?) and supports it. |
| 3. A proposed rule | One added line on the exit ticket: "Write one rule you would cut, change, or add, and say why." | A rule stated clearly enough to enforce, with a reason that refers to the reading. |
| 4. Discussion | Observer organizer, including the tally of times a student built on someone else's idea. Before-and-after temperature check, with a count of who moved and why. | Builds on a peer by name, cites a rule by letter, and can say what moved them (or why they held). |

I grade evidence and honesty, not the position landed on. "I changed my mind because of what someone said" is the best possible answer. The temperature-check count and the observer organizers are the artifacts to show anyone who asks what students did.
