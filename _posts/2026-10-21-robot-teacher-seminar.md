A school district bought a humanoid robot to help teach high school coding and robotics. Then the state stepped in. This is a seminar about whether a school should buy it. It is a decision, not a debate over who is right, and your students are the ones affected.

The format is in the [Socratic Seminar Kit](/socratic-seminar-kit/?topic=A%20Robot%20for%20Room%20114&exit=Name%20one%20piece%20of%20student%20data%20this%20robot%20would%20hold%2C%20and%20one%20risk%20of%20keeping%20it.): the norms, the fishbowl, and the three tickets. What changes here is the reading, which arrives in two parts, and a third round that is spoken, not handed out.

My own take is in [AI Robot Teachers Are Almost Here]({% post_url 2026-07-24-robot-teacher %}). It stays out of the room until the end.

## Learning goals

By the end of the seminar, students can:

1. Weigh the beneficial and harmful effects of a computing innovation, including effects its builders did not intend, and defend a position with evidence from the reading. AP CSP: [IOC-1.A](https://learn.porttack.com/standards/apcsp-standards-reference.html#IOC-1.A), [IOC-1.B](https://learn.porttack.com/standards/apcsp-standards-reference.html#IOC-1.B).
2. Explain how unequal access to technology affects who benefits from it, using this district as the example. AP CSP: [IOC-1.C](https://learn.porttack.com/standards/apcsp-standards-reference.html#IOC-1.C).
3. Identify what personal data a school system like this would collect, and the privacy risks of keeping it. AP CSP: [IOC-2.A](https://learn.porttack.com/standards/apcsp-standards-reference.html#IOC-2.A).
4. Discuss as a group: cite the text, build on what others said, and change a position when the evidence changes.

## The run of show

Rough minutes, scaled to your period. The structure is the [kit's run of show](/socratic-seminar-kit/#the-run-of-show) with a second reading and a third round.

1. **Norms, out loud (5 min).** Post them, read them, hold to them.
2. **Opening temperature check (3 min).** Students stand on a line. One wall: "A robot tutor is just another tool, like a textbook or a calculator." The other: "A tutor with a face is different."
3. **Read part 1 (3 min).** Then the entry ticket, or students use one already filled out.
4. **Round 1, the pitch (15 min).** Opening question to every student, briefly: "You are the school board. What would you need to see before you vote yes?" Then fishbowl with the hot seat.
5. **Round 2, the objections (10 min).** Hand out part 2. Run the temperature check again. Who moved, and what moved them?
6. **Round 3, spoken only (5 min).** See below.
7. **Closing temperature check (3 min).** One last move to the line.
8. **Reveal and exit ticket (10 min).** Your take as one opinion among many, then they write.

## Round 3: one more fact, said out loud

This is deliberately not on a handout. Hand out nothing, show no images, and say it once, plainly, in your own words. Something like:

> One more thing. I'm telling you this one instead of handing it to you. The company that makes the robot bought another company in 2024. That company makes sex dolls and sex robots. The company says its education team is a separate team, that it shares no employees, offices, or technology with that business, and that it planned to split the two companies apart. I'm not telling you what to do with that. I'm asking: does it matter, and if so, why?

Then stop talking.

Why this works as a round, and not as a shock:

- It comes after they have formed a view, so it tests that view instead of replacing it.
- The question is about trust, not the product: should a vendor's history affect whether you believe its promises? That is an AP CSP question. The district's whole safety case ("no access to personally identifiable student information," "closed system") is a set of promises from the vendor.
- Say it once and move on. The performer who wants a reaction gets nothing to push against. If a student laughs or makes a joke, treat it like any norm break: name it once, calmly, and return to the question.
- Keep the same facts the company's own statement gives. Do not add details.

Questions for round 3, back-pocket:

- Does it change your vote? Should it?
- If the company says its education team is separate, what would you need to see to believe it?
- Is it fair to judge a product by the history of the company that owns it? When is it fair, and when is it not?
- Would your answer change if the earlier business were something else, like a weapons maker or a tobacco company?

## Facilitator questions

Back-pocket only. The entry ticket asks students to write their own.

**Round 1:**

- The district's argument is that banning technology is the easy way out. Is it?
- If you were a superintendent of a small, poor district, would you want to be first, or wait and see?
- What would "it worked" look like? The district said it would judge success mostly by feedback from students and teachers. Is that enough?

**CS lens (the AP CSP questions):**

- Each student gets an ID, and the robot remembers past conversations. What data is that? Who could see it?
- "No access to personally identifiable student information." What counts as personally identifiable? Could a conversation history identify you without your name?
- The robot flags words like self-harm and alerts administrators. Who decides what gets flagged? What happens to a student who sees that coming? (This connects directly to [the chatbot-and-principal provision]({% post_url 2026-08-08-what-happens-when-the-chatbot-tells-the-principal %}) of the STUDENTS FIRST Act.)
- The district is poorer than most. Does this narrow the gap between rich and poor schools, or widen it?
- Could a robot tutor be biased? In what ways?

**Round 2:**

- The Commissioner is "concerned about the effect that a humanoid robot may have." She does not say what effect. What might she mean?
- The district paused instead of canceling. What does "enhanced student data privacy agreements" need to say?

**After the reveal:**

- Where am I wrong?

## Evidence of learning

All of this is collected during the seminar. Nothing needs a separate quiz.

| Goal | Evidence | What meeting it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Effects | Entry ticket: a starting side and two pieces of evidence by paragraph number. Exit ticket: what they believed, what they believe now. | A position that names at least one benefit and one harm, and cites the reading. |
| 2. Access | Round 1 and 2 talk, tracked on the observer organizer. Exit ticket. | Connects the district's size, income, or location to why it wanted the robot, or to who else could afford one. |
| 3. Personal data | One added line on the exit ticket: "Name one piece of student data this robot would hold, and one risk of keeping it." | A specific data type (student ID, conversation history, flagged-term alerts) and a specific risk. |
| 4. Discussion | Observer organizer, including the tally of times a student built on someone else's idea. Three temperature checks, with a count of who moved and why. | Builds on a peer by name, cites a paragraph, and can say what moved them (or why they held). |

I grade evidence and honesty, not the position landed on. "I changed my mind because of what someone said" is the best possible answer. The temperature-check count and the observer organizers are the artifacts to show anyone who asks what students did.

## Standards

Common Core ELA (California adoption): SL.6.1 / SL.9-10.1, SL.6.3 / SL.9-10.3, RI.6.1 / RI.9-10.1, RI.6.8 / RI.9-10.8, W.6.1 / W.9-10.1. What each one covers is in the [kit](/socratic-seminar-kit/#common-core-and-california-mandates).

**AP CSP, Big Idea 5, Impact of Computing.** This seminar touches:

- **5.1 Beneficial and Harmful Effects.** The district's case against the objections.
- **5.2 Digital Divide.** A poor, rural district's chance to get new technology first.
- **5.3 Computing Bias.** What a tutor trained on data might get wrong.
- **5.5 Legal and Ethical Concerns.** The student-data agreements and who is responsible.
- **5.6 Safe Computing.** The robot's student IDs, stored conversations, and flagged-term alerts.

Check these topic labels against the current College Board framework before citing them formally.
