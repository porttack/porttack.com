[Rumman Chowdhury argues in MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/08/20/1142571/ai-consciousness-debate-trap/) that the AI consciousness debate is a trap. If AI is seen as too advanced to control, the companies that build it can't be held liable for the harm it causes.

<!--more-->

Her argument runs like this. Tech leaders talk about "superhuman" systems that need regulating. A separate group of philosophers asks whether AI might be a "moral patient" that deserves legal protection. The goals look opposed, but both describe an AI that acts on its own, and that picture matters in court.

The dozens of lawsuits against AI companies now (self-harm, nonconsensual images, copyright, psychosis) mostly argue that a company shipped a product without enough safeguards. If AI were a legal person, she says, likely modeled on corporate personhood, a defense lawyer could argue the AI "employee" went rogue. She calls this moral outsourcing, a term she coined in 2018. The idea would move from a way of talking to a legal strategy.

> Legal personhood exists to grant protection. The question to ask is, protection for whom—or for what?

She doesn't dismiss the empathy argument. It's persuasive, she says. Her answer is that AI is corporate-built software, and when it harms people, the cause is companies that were negligent in a rush to hit revenue targets.

I spent about four decades writing software, and I agree with her. A program doesn't go rogue. A bug has an owner, and so does the decision to ship it. Whether a model is conscious is an interesting question, but it's a different question from who is responsible when it hurts someone, and I don't want the first one used to dodge the second.

When a headline says "the AI did it," I want my students to ask who built it, who deployed it, and who profits from it.
