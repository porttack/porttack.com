I have twins, a boy and a girl. In second grade I signed my daughter up for [Mini Mermaids](https://www.minimermaidrunningclub.org/about-us), a running program built around teaching girls to quiet the negative self-talk. Good program. I did not think through the part where my son noticed.

<!--more-->

He was six, and he went straight at the parent volunteer running it to ask how they could leave boys out. Explaining that life is unfair is hard enough at six. It's harder when his sister is visibly getting the better deal.

So the week before third grade started, while I was cleaning out the PTA closet, I ran into the new principal. "Hello, Mrs. Lahey, my name is Eric. This year I'm starting Young Tritons, a running program for boys." Then I told her what Mini Mermaids did and what I thought I could do with boys.

Six months later, after training hard enough to keep up with third graders, plus marketing, recruiting, and finding an assistant coach, I was standing on the soccer field with 23 boys. Any teacher can tell you how a badly lopsided group is going to go. I had 23 of them.

They got to work immediately. Most of them couldn't walk past a stick without picking it up and pointing it at a friend. By the fifth session I'd learned that if I couldn't find Ashton on the field, I should look up. He climbed everything. We ended up paying one M&M per completed lap.

What actually worried me was the wrestling. Turn around for five seconds and two or three of them would be on the ground, sometimes my own kid. So I went looking and found an article in Psychology Today about boys and play fighting.

The next session I asked them straight out: when you wrestle, is it because you're mad at each other, or is it for fun? "Yes, coach, we just love to play."

Fine. New rules. If you're going to play fight you need a safe place to do it, and if anyone says "bananas," everything stops and everyone checks that everyone else is okay. Within seconds I had eight pairs of boys on the grass, laughing and tugging, doing what boys have done forever.

**It was as if I had unwittingly created a fight club.** Mrs. Lahey had already gone home for the day. The first rule of fight club is that nobody talks about fight club, but these were third graders and their well-being was my responsibility. At the next session I told all of them there would be no physical contact, period.

A lot of good things happened too.

- The boys took turns running a water station for everyone doing laps.
- They got to run on the high school track and see what's ahead of them.
- We took a group photo at Henry Cowell before our practice race that I still think about.
- My youngest boy ran the final race with his older sister. That picture became the marketing photo for Santa Cruz Young Tritons the next year.

What third grade taught me:

- Every session took more preparation, and more worrying, than it looked like from the outside.
- The joy those boys had when they got to run and play is worth paying attention to.
- Their growth was the whole payment.
- I got a small look at why my mother loved teaching so much.
