Would the students vote yes on a robot teacher? A real school district in rural New York agreed to spend $57,590 on one, a humanoid robot to help teach high school coding and robotics. In this seminar the students are the school board, and the decision leads to a bigger question: **Can robotic AI teachers replace human teachers?**

Three rounds on the same decision: the reading, a closer look with a photo and two videos, and one more fact in a short clip. Every student also prepares the best argument for the side they did not pick, and *you can ask a few to defend the position they disagree with.*

**The format.** A fishbowl: a small circle discusses while the rest of the class observes and fills in an organizer, then the groups swap. An empty chair, the hot seat, lets an observer join for one comment and then leave. Students follow five posted norms, read aloud at the start. Four CS-lens questions are on a slide for anyone to use at any time: Each student has an ID, so what data is that? Could a chat history identify you by itself? Who decides which words get flagged? Does this narrow the gap, or widen it? The class also starts by choosing one of three corners, and the entry and exit tickets record where each student starts and ends on the buy-or-don't question.

**At a glance.**

- **Class time:** about an hour. The core run below is 55 minutes, and the optional items add about 20 more.
- **Prep:** about 10 minutes. Click through the slides and watch the news clip, the avatar interview, and the Round 3 clip.
- **Print:** the handout (double-sided) and the reading. Role cards and the next-day worksheet are optional.

**A note on content.** In Round 2, the avatar interview touches bullying and a student bringing a gun to school. Round 3 reveals that the robot's maker is tied to sex dolls. The question in Round 3 is about trust, not shock. High school students have likely met these topics already.

## Learning goals

By the end of the seminar, students can:

1. Weigh the beneficial and harmful effects of a computing innovation, including effects its builders did not intend, and defend a position with evidence from the reading. AP CSP: IOC-1.A, IOC-1.B.
2. Discuss how unequal access to technology affects who benefits from it, using this district as the example. AP CSP: IOC-1.C.
3. Discuss what personal data a school system like this would collect, and the privacy risks of keeping it. AP CSP: IOC-2.A.
4. Discuss as a group: cite the text, build on what others said, state the best argument for the side they disagree with, and change a position when the evidence changes.

## Round 3: one more fact

<iframe src="https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/8aXiTqdyHmQ?start=11&end=28&rel=0&modestbranding=1&playsinline=1" title="The 17-second Round 3 clip (11 to 28 seconds)" width="240" height="135" loading="lazy" allow="encrypted-media; picture-in-picture" allowfullscreen style="float: right; border: 0; margin: 0.2em 0 0.5em 1em;"></iframe>

Round 3 reveals that the robot's maker is tied to sex dolls. Realbotix bought Simulcra, the parent company of RealDoll, in April 2024, and RealDoll makes sex dolls and sex robots ([New York Focus](https://nysfocus.com/2026/07/14/new-york-humanoid-robot-teacher-salamanca-school-district)). Realbotix says its education team is separate: no shared employees, payroll, locations, or technology. After Round 2, "One More Thing" leads to a 17-second clip (11 to 28 seconds) from a news-comedy segment on the story, then "Still Buy It?" opens one more discussion on the same decision. You can swap people or keep the circle.

**Why this works as a round, and not as a shock:**

- It comes after they have formed a view, so it tests that view instead of replacing it.
- The question is about trust, not the product: should a vendor's history affect whether you believe its promises? That is an AP CSP question. The district's whole safety case ("no access to personally identifiable student information," "closed system") is a set of promises from the vendor.
- Show it once and move on. Do not add details beyond what the clip says. If a student laughs or makes a joke, treat it like any norm break: name it once, calmly, and return to the question.

The "Still Buy It?" slide already asks whether it changes your answer and whether a company's history should change how much you believe its promises. Two back-pocket questions, if the room needs them:

- If the company says its education team is separate, what would you need to see to believe it?
- Would your answer change if the earlier business were something else, like a weapons maker or a tobacco company?

## Facilitator questions

Back-pocket only. The entry ticket asks students to write their own.

**Round 1:**

- The union says, "We want real human relationships and for our students to learn how to form [real human relationships] organically." The superintendent says banning technology is the easy way out. Which do you agree with, and why?
- If you were a superintendent of a small, poor district, would you want to be first, or wait and see?
- What would "it worked" look like? The district said it would judge success mostly by feedback from students and teachers. Is that enough?

**CS lens (the AP CSP questions):**

- Each student gets an ID, and the robot remembers past conversations. What data is that? Who could see it?
- "No access to personally identifiable student information." What counts as personally identifiable? Could a conversation history identify you without your name?
- The robot flags words like self-harm and alerts administrators. Who decides what gets flagged? What happens to a student who sees that coming? (This connects directly to [the chatbot-and-principal provision]({% post_url 2026-08-08-what-happens-when-the-chatbot-tells-the-principal %}) of the STUDENTS FIRST Act.)
- The district is poorer than most. Does this narrow the gap between rich and poor schools, or widen it?
- Could a robot teacher be biased? In what ways?

**Round 2 (the photo and the videos):**

Same main question as Round 1. The rest are back-pocket prompts.

- Who is each person speaking for: the superintendent, the union, the company?
- What if the robot looked different: an old man, say, instead of a young woman? Would your answer change? Who decided what she should look like, and why?
- The district says the robot will not replace teachers, and the union says students need real human relationships. Is there anything they agree on?
- The superintendent said words like self-harm alert administrators. The avatar says a teacher cannot "just casually read" a student's conversations. Are those the same claim? What would you want clarified?
- When asked who is better at math, boys or girls, the avatar gives a good answer. Is saying the right thing enough for a tutor? How would you test whether it is biased?
- When a student mentions a gun, the avatar tells them to report it to an adult. Should it also tell an adult itself? What would students lose if it did?
- When the avatar answers a student, who is responsible for what it says: the district, the company, or the robot?

## Playing the other side (optional)

The entry ticket's fourth question already asks every student for the best argument for the side they did not pick, so everyone prepares the other side. This is the line to grade, for how well thought out it is and not for whether the student agrees. The handout scores it out of 2, and [the kit](/socratic-seminar-kit/#playing-the-other-side) has a fuller rubric.

If a show of hands on buy or don't is lopsided, you can also assign one or two students to defend the side most of the room does not hold, in the Round 2 fishbowl:

- **Who.** Ask for volunteers first. If nobody volunteers, ask students from the larger side, who are then arguing against their own pick. Do not pick the most outspoken.
- **When.** Round 2, after the swap, so Round 1 stays authentic. Say out loud who is playing a role.
- **The cards.** [Print the role cards](/socratic-seminar-cards/?topic=Would%20You%20Vote%20for%20a%20Robot%20Teacher%3F&a=Buy%20it&ap=Banning%20technology%20is%20the%20easy%20way%20out.%20Schools%20should%20teach%20students%20to%20use%20it%20well.%20%28Reading%2C%20paragraph%206%29%0AThe%20robot%20runs%20on%20a%20closed%20system%2C%20and%20the%20company%20will%20not%20have%20access%20to%20personally%20identifiable%20student%20information.%20%28Paragraph%205%29%0AIt%20is%20a%20tutor%20for%20AI%20and%20robotics%20classes%2C%20and%20an%20avatar%20students%20could%20use%20after%20school.%20%28Paragraph%203%29&b=Do%20not%20buy%20it&bp=Students%20need%20real%20human%20relationships%2C%20not%20simulated%20ones.%20%28Paragraph%207%29%0AThe%20robot%20would%20keep%20each%20student%27s%20past%20conversations%20under%20their%20ID.%20That%20is%20a%20lot%20of%20personal%20data.%20%28Paragraph%204%29%0AThe%20privacy%20promises%20are%20the%20superintendent%27s%20words.%20Nothing%20in%20the%20reading%20says%20anyone%20has%20checked%20them.). The six points come only from the reading, so every student can check them.
- **Afterward.** The handout's second exit question asks, "If you argued a side you did not pick, what was that like?" In Round 2, you can also ask the room, "Who heard a strong argument for the side you didn't pick?"

## The reveal

**What actually happened (optional).** It is held back to the end on purpose: saying what the district did before students decide would bias them. Slide 23 shows it.

- The state's Education Commissioner, Betty Rosa, wrote to the superintendent on July 24: "the Department remains concerned about the effect that a humanoid robot may have within a high school classroom." ([New York Focus](https://nysfocus.com/2026/07/24/new-york-robot-teacher-plan-paused))
- The same day, the district announced the project was on hold while it works "through enhanced student data privacy agreements" with the state and talks with the community. It has not said when it might restart.
- On July 29, New York State Sen. Shelley Mayer introduced a bill, [S10671](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/S10671), which would require every school district, charter school, and BOCES in New York to adopt a policy prohibiting AI-enabled humanoid robots "in an instructional capacity." As of October 24, 2026 it is in committee in the New York Senate and has not passed either chamber. It is a New York bill and would not apply anywhere else.
- If time allows, ask: "A New York senator is asking the same question the students just answered. Does a ban answer it?"
- **Compare (optional).** The last slide sets the New York bill beside your own district's policy. For example, a clause added to one district's union contract in 2026 lets the union veto any attempt to replace teachers' jobs with AI. Swap in your own district's policy. Ask: "How are these alike? How are they different?"

## Next day: a short writing assignment (optional)

Students write one paragraph on [a printable worksheet](/robot-policy-worksheet/): the rule your district should adopt about humanoid robots in classrooms, and who decides. The rule should say whether a robot may replace an aide, replace a teacher, or neither. The Compare slide gives two models, a state ban and a union contract clause. The paragraph names one thing the rule protects and one thing it gives up. Read two or three aloud and ask what each would have changed about the district's vote.

The rubric is printed on the worksheet, so students know how it is graded. The rule they choose is not graded, only the thinking and the writing.

| Item | Points |
|---|---|
| Legible: I can read all of it | 1 |
| Complete sentences | 1 |
| The rule (aide, teacher, or neither), and who decides | 1 |
| One thing the rule protects | 1 |
| One thing the rule gives up | 1 |
| A detail from the reading, a video, or the discussion | 1 |

## Evidence of learning

All of this is collected during the seminar. Nothing needs a separate quiz.

| Goal | Evidence | What meeting it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Effects | Entry ticket: a starting side and two pieces of evidence by paragraph number. Exit ticket: where they started and ended. | A position that names at least one benefit and one harm, and cites the reading. |
| 2. Access | Round 1 and 2 talk (listen for it), and the next-day paragraph if you assign it. | Connects the district's size, income, or location to why it wanted the robot, or to who else could afford one. |
| 3. Personal data | One added line on the exit ticket: "Name one piece of student data this robot would hold, and one risk of keeping it." | A specific data type (student ID, conversation history, flagged-term alerts) and a specific risk. |
| 4. Discussion | Observer organizer: the tally of times a student built on someone's idea. Exit ticket Start and End lines: count who moved and why. Entry ticket question 4, scored out of 2 for how well thought out it is (fuller rubric in [the kit](/socratic-seminar-kit/#playing-the-other-side)). | Builds on a peer by name, cites a paragraph, can say what moved them (or why they held), and states the other side fairly. |

The handout prints a 6-point rubric on the front, so students know how it is graded: the entry ticket is worth 4 points (two questions and a side with a reason, two pieces of evidence, and the other side for 2), the observer organizer 1, and the exit ticket 1. Blank or unreadable answers earn no points, and I fill in the score column.

I grade evidence and honesty, not the position landed on. "I changed my mind because of what someone said" is the best possible answer. I check goals 2 and 3 but do not grade them: I skim the data line on the exit ticket and sort the sheets into specific, vague, and blank, which shows me what to revisit. The Start-to-End count and the observer organizers are the artifacts to show anyone who asks what students did.
